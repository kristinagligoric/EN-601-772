Format Overview

  • This is an advanced graduate class built around student-led discussion of recent research papers and an original semester-long research project. Each session is led by a student and facilitated by the instructor. The goal is to develop the ability to read new work critically, to argue about methods and evidence, and to carry that judgment into your own research.

Class Structure and Weekly Flow

  • Before each session: readings and a written reflection
    • Readings: 2-3 research papers per session. Come prepared to discuss them in detail, not just in summary.
    • Reading reflection: A short written critique, due by end of day before each session (see the schedule for exact dates). Reflections are written from an assigned perspective or role: for example, a positive reviewer, replicator, skeptic, practitioner, or author of a competing approach. Roles rotate over the semester.
    • Reflections feed directly into class: the discussion lead builds prompts from them, so submitting on time is important!
  • In class: student-led discussion
    • Short presentation (roughly 15-20 min): The discussion lead contextualizes the papers: the problem it addresses, where it sits relative to prior work, what is actually new, and what the evidence does and does not support. The discussion lead identifies at least one additional reading relevant to the topic (a paper, news article, policy brief, etc.).
    • Discussion prompts: The lead prepares questions drawn from the class reflections, surfacing points of disagreement.
    • Guided discussion: The lead runs the conversation and the instructor facilitates.
    • Attendance: Attendance and active participation are mandatory.
  • Discussion leadership
    • Each student leads the discussion once over the semester. Sign-ups happen in the first weeks of class.
    • Leads are encouraged to reach out to the instructor and meet with the instructor beforehand to talk through the framing and the prompts.
  • Active participation
    • Participation means raising methodological questions, connecting papers to broader themes across the course, and bringing in perspective from your own research. Sitting quietly is not participation.

Semester-Long Research Project

  • Students work individually or in pairs on a topic of their own choosing. The starting point is a relevant research paper from outside the course reading list: you select it, situate it, and propose a study that applies, validates, or critically evaluates AI methods for social science research.
  • The project runs in stages: a literature review, a written proposal with an in-class proposal presentation, and a final presentation with a conference-style report of 4 pages, with potential for publication.
  • Deadlines for each stage are listed in the schedule.

Course Deliverables and Grade Breakdown

  • Reading responses: 25%
  • Class participation (including discussion leadership): 25%
  • Project literature review and proposal: 15%
  • Project proposal presentation: 15%
  • Project final presentation and paper: 20%
Grading Scale: A+ (98-100), A (94-97), A− (90-93), B+ (87-89), B (83-86), B− (80-82), C+ (77-79), C (73-76), C− (70-72), D+ (67-69), D (63-66), F (below 63). Class participation includes contributing substantively to discussion, engaging with other students' arguments, being prepared to discuss the assigned readings, and leading discussion for your assigned papers.

Absences

Each student has up to three free cards that may be used to miss an entire class, including the reading reflection for that session, without penalty. Free cards do not apply to sessions you are scheduled to lead, or to project deadlines and presentations. Students are expected to notify the instructor in advance when absent and using a free card. No explanation is required. If you need to swap a discussion leadership slot, contact the instructor as early as you can so the schedule can be rearranged.

Late submissions

Late submissions are accepted with a penalty of 25% per 24-hour period after the deadline. Submissions more than three days late may not be accepted. Reading reflections are an exception: because they feed into the next session's discussion, they cannot be submitted late and should be covered with a free card instead. Please plan accordingly.

Why this workload?

The workload is designed around sustained engagement with a fast-moving research literature rather than high-pressure performance. Weekly reading and short written critiques are what make a seminar discussion worth having, and the role-playing is there to push you past agreeing with whatever you read most recently. Deeper evaluation is concentrated in the research project, which is meant to leave you with something you could actually continue working on and submit.
